# 04 — Typography, Imagery, Product Presentation, and Visual Storytelling

<aside>
🔤

Typography communicates voice; imagery communicates evidence, emotion, context, and action. They must tell the same story rather than behaving as separate decorations.

</aside>

## 1. Typography begins with the brand

Use the brand’s approved typefaces first. If none exist, select by communication need:

- **Geometric sans serif:** modern, technology, finance, direct consumer brands.
- **Humanist sans serif:** friendly, educational, service-led, accessible.
- **Grotesk sans serif:** neutral, editorial, contemporary.
- **Serif:** premium, cultural, editorial, heritage, thoughtful.
- **Rounded display:** youthful, food, playful consumer brands.
- **Condensed display:** urgency, events, sport, strong promotional impact.
- **Script or handwritten:** personal, expressive, celebratory; use sparingly.

Do not use a font because it is fashionable if its personality conflicts with the brand.

## 2. Typeface limits

Usually use:

- One family with several weights; or
- One display family plus one supporting family.

A third style should appear only when it performs a clear role, such as a price, badge, or campaign accent. Too many type personalities fracture brand recognition.

## 3. Text hierarchy

Build a dependable type ladder:

1. Headline.
2. Secondary phrase or offer.
3. Supporting explanation.
4. CTA.
5. Utility details.

Hierarchy may be created through size, weight, case, contrast, spacing, and containment. Avoid making every line bold.

## 4. Headline construction

- Keep the main idea compact.
- Break lines by meaning, not simply by available width.
- Protect important phrases from awkward separation.
- Use tighter leading for large display text.
- Avoid widows: one short word stranded on a final line.
- Use emphasis on one or two important words, not every word.
- Keep punctuation consistent with the brand voice.

Headline styling should reinforce meaning: speed may use forward motion; premium messaging may use restrained spacing; playful food messaging may use irregular display rhythm.

## 5. Supporting copy

- Use short lines and plain language.
- Keep line length comfortable.
- Align with the headline system.
- Increase leading as type becomes smaller.
- Avoid placing long copy over busy imagery.
- Do not repeat the headline without adding information.

## 6. Readability rules

- Maintain sufficient value contrast.
- Avoid thin type over detailed photographs.
- Do not stretch type disproportionately.
- Limit strong outlines, glows, and shadows.
- Use uppercase carefully; it reduces word-shape recognition in long text.
- Test phone numbers, dates, prices, and addresses at output size.

## 7. Image role selection

Every main image should perform one primary role:

- **Hero:** attracts attention.
- **Proof:** shows the real product, interface, result, or person.
- **Context:** shows where and how the offer is used.
- **Emotion:** creates aspiration, joy, trust, urgency, or empathy.
- **Explanation:** demonstrates a process, feature, or transformation.
- **Metaphor:** visualises an abstract promise such as speed, reach, or freedom.

A strong campaign may combine roles, but KO OS should know which role dominates.

## 8. Choosing the subject

Select a subject whose identity, clothing, age range, action, and environment fit the audience and message. Do not use a generic smiling model when the design needs product demonstration, professional authority, cultural specificity, or emotional nuance.

For people, define:

- Expression.
- Gaze direction.
- Body orientation.
- Hand action.
- Clothing category.
- Crop.
- Camera height.
- Relationship to product or text.

## 9. Camera-angle logic

- **Eye level:** direct, trustworthy, familiar.
- **Slightly high angle:** organised overview, gentle, useful for tables and product arrangements.
- **Low angle:** confidence, energy, power, aspiration.
- **Three-quarter angle:** reveals form, packaging depth, and product texture.
- **Top-down:** orderly collections, food spreads, flat lays.
- **Close-up:** texture, emotion, product detail.
- **Wide environmental view:** context and storytelling.

Choose the angle for meaning. Dramatic perspective without communication purpose becomes a gimmick.

## 10. Gaze and directional flow

- A person looking toward the headline sends attention to the message.
- An extended hand can connect the subject to the product or CTA.
- A product tilted toward the centre prevents energy from leaving the canvas.
- Objects moving toward the CTA create a natural completion path.

Avoid poses that direct the viewer off-canvas unless another element deliberately brings the eye back.

## 11. Cropping

Crop intentionally:

- Do not cut through important joints awkwardly.
- Preserve hands when they interact with products.
- Keep the face large enough to communicate expression.
- Allow oversized foreground objects to exit the canvas confidently.
- Do not crop product labels or distinguishing features.

A crop may create energy, but it must not damage recognition.

## 12. Product presentation

The product should appear as evidence, not a small afterthought.

Methods:

- Enlarge it as the hero.
- Hold it close to the camera.
- Place it on a platform.
- Use a controlled cluster for product range.
- Show interface screens clearly.
- Repeat packaging at different scales.
- Use ingredients or functional objects as supporting context.

For multiple products, establish one hero and subordinate the rest through size, distance, or lower contrast.

## 13. Floating-object systems

Floating objects are useful for technology, youth, entertainment, and abundance.

Rules:

- Arrange along one curve, diagonal, orbit, or perspective path.
- Vary scale to indicate depth.
- Keep shadows and light direction consistent.
- Avoid equal spacing and equal size.
- Protect text and faces.
- Limit the number of hero-level objects.

## 14. Lifestyle scenes

A lifestyle scene should show a believable use case. Connect the model to the product through touch, gaze, body direction, or environment.

Use foreground, middle ground, and background to tell a compact story. The brand product should remain visible enough that the image does not become generic stock photography.

## 15. Image-generation instructions

A useful image prompt should specify:

- Subject and identity requirements.
- Action and expression.
- Product or object interaction.
- Camera angle and lens feeling.
- Crop and framing.
- Light direction and quality.
- Background context.
- Required negative space.
- Intended placement in the final design.
- Realism and texture requirements.
- Elements that must not appear.

Generate for the layout. If text belongs on the left, direct the subject to the right and preserve a clean left region.

## 16. Typography–image relationship

Choose one relationship:

- Side-by-side separation.
- Text above image.
- Text inside a protected image zone.
- Image breaking around the headline.
- Product replacing a letter or acting as punctuation.
- Text following a scene perspective.

Do not use advanced overlap if a simple separation communicates better.

## Correction rules

- If generic: increase contextual specificity and product interaction.
- If artificial: correct lighting, texture, hands, perspective, and contact shadows.
- If type feels detached: align it to the scene or repeat a shape/angle from the imagery.
- If too many fonts: return to the brand family and create hierarchy with weight and size.
- If the product disappears: increase scale, edge contrast, or isolation.

## Core rule

> The headline states the promise; the imagery should prove, dramatise, or contextualise it.
