# 05 — Footer, CTA, Contact Information, and Lower-Third Systems

<aside>
🧩

A footer is a functional conclusion to the design. It should collect action and utility information, ground the composition, and support recognition without becoming a second headline.

</aside>

## 1. Determine whether a footer is needed

Use a footer when the design contains contact information, download actions, sponsor marks, address, social handles, terms, or a recurring campaign signature.

A footer may be unnecessary when:

- The post is a simple greeting or awareness message.
- The CTA already appears clearly in the main composition.
- Utility information would overcrowd the canvas.
- Platform context supplies the action outside the image.

“No footer” is a valid design decision, not an unfinished state.

## 2. Footer information hierarchy

Arrange footer information in this order:

1. Main action or primary contact.
2. Secondary contact or website.
3. Social handle.
4. Address, delivery area, terms, or sponsor marks.

Use icons to aid scanning, not to replace ambiguous text.

## 3. Full-width straight footer

**Geometry:** rectangle spanning the full width; top edge horizontal at 0°; square or slightly rounded upper corners.

**Use for:** corporate services, schools, recruitment, campaigns with several contact items.

**Strength:** stable, readable, easy to repeat.

**Watch:** it can feel heavy. Keep height proportional and add sufficient padding.

## 4. Floating rounded contact bar

**Geometry:** rounded rectangle inset from both sides; horizontal top and bottom edges; generous corner radius; may sit over the background or a shallow backing panel.

**Use for:** modern services, technology, lifestyle, concise contact information.

**Strength:** feels like an interface component and keeps utility information grouped.

**Watch:** do not squeeze too many items into a short pill.

## 5. Ribbon or trapezoid footer

**Geometry:** floating strip with angled side cuts, commonly 8–15°; top and bottom mostly horizontal; may taper or extend behind the subject.

**Use for:** schools, events, celebrations, youthful promotions.

**Strength:** adds motion without occupying the full base.

**Watch:** align text to a stable inner rectangle; do not rotate every line to match the sides.

## 6. Angled split footer

**Geometry:** two or more panels meeting at a diagonal seam, commonly 10–20° from vertical; full-width or floating.

**Use for:** separating phone from social handle, two departments, two actions, or two brand colours.

**Strength:** creates energy and clear grouping.

**Watch:** preserve equal optical padding around each group. The seam should not pass through text or icons.

## 7. Curved-shoulder footer

**Geometry:** full-width lower band whose upper edge rises behind a model or product in one or more broad curves.

**Use for:** lifestyle, youth, technology, product-led campaigns.

**Strength:** connects the footer to the hero instead of creating a hard separation.

**Watch:** curves must be broad and intentional. Small repeated humps appear decorative and weak.

## 8. Extended rounded panel

**Geometry:** a large rounded rectangle begins near the lower edge and may continue beyond the canvas; only its upper portion is visible.

**Use for:** simple CTA-led designs, greetings, modern retail posts.

**Strength:** creates a clean action zone while maintaining openness.

**Watch:** ensure the visible portion contains enough internal padding.

## 9. Text-only footer

**Geometry:** no container; contact details sit directly on a quiet continuation of the background.

**Use for:** minimal service flyers, premium editorial layouts, designs with calm lower space.

**Strength:** light and unobtrusive.

**Watch:** it requires strong contrast and disciplined spacing. Do not place it over detailed imagery.

## 10. Darkened-background footer

**Geometry:** no separate shape; the background gradually darkens or simplifies toward the bottom.

**Use for:** monochromatic campaigns, lifestyle images, designs where a separate bar would interrupt flow.

**Strength:** integrates utility information into the scene.

**Watch:** the tonal transition must be sufficient for readability but subtle enough to avoid looking like an accidental shadow.

## 11. Card/footer hybrid

**Geometry:** one or more small cards float at the bottom, sometimes connected to the CTA or service cards.

**Use for:** multiple contact options, schedules, benefits, addresses, or app actions.

**Strength:** modular and easy to scan.

**Watch:** maintain equal height, corner radius, padding, and icon scale.

## 12. Product-stage footer

**Geometry:** a lower platform, pedestal, shelf, or surface carries products while contact information sits in front of or beside it.

**Use for:** food, retail, gadgets, cosmetics, and product-range campaigns.

**Strength:** lets the product overlap the lower system and creates depth.

**Watch:** preserve product labels and keep utility text unobstructed.

## 13. Multi-level footer

**Geometry:** CTA on an upper strip; contacts on a lower strip; may include a product overlap between levels.

**Use for:** designs with a strong campaign action and several utility items.

**Strength:** separates conversion from reference information.

**Watch:** keep the total height under control so the footer does not consume the composition.

## 14. Footer selection rules

- **Several contact groups:** straight full-width or multi-level.
- **One phone number + one handle:** floating rounded bar.
- **Energetic youth design:** ribbon, angled split, or curved shoulder.
- **Premium minimal design:** text-only footer.
- **Full-scene lifestyle image:** darkened-background footer.
- **Strong product range:** product-stage footer.
- **App or technology brand:** interface-style rounded panel.
- **Campaign needing repeatability:** standardise one footer component and reuse its geometry.

## 15. Subject and footer overlap

A model or product may overlap the footer to create depth.

Rules:

- Overlap the upper edge, not the utility text.
- Keep feet, hands, or products from appearing accidentally cut by the bar.
- Place the contact panel in front when readability is critical.
- Use a contact shadow if the subject appears to sit on the footer.
- Avoid more than one competing overlap pattern.

## 16. CTA systems

A CTA can be:

- Rounded button.
- Compact pill.
- Bordered label.
- Sticker or badge.
- Arrow-linked text.
- App-store badges.
- Phone/WhatsApp action.
- Website capsule.

The CTA should use an action verb and remain concise. Its shape, corner radius, icon style, and wording can become recurring brand assets.

## 17. Footer alignment

- Align all icons to one optical centre.
- Keep icon-to-text gaps equal.
- Use consistent divider length and weight.
- Maintain one text baseline where possible.
- Keep phone numbers grouped correctly.
- Do not mix centred and left-aligned contact groups without a structural reason.

## 18. Footer sizing

The footer should be subordinate to the main message but readable at actual size. If utility text requires a large footer, reduce the number of items, shorten labels, or move secondary details outside the design.

## 19. Footer consistency for brands

A repeated footer can stabilise varied campaign layouts. Preserve:

- Geometry.
- Height.
- Corner radius.
- Internal order.
- CTA wording.
- Icon family.
- Spacing.
- Relationship to the canvas edge.

Allow limited variation in content length and overlap, not a complete redesign on every post.

## 20. Failure checks

Revise if:

- The footer looks added after the design was completed.
- Contact text is too small.
- The footer competes with the headline.
- The edge shape has no relationship to the wider composition.
- The model blocks contact information.
- Dividers create more clutter than structure.
- A complex footer is used where no footer is needed.

## Core rule

> The footer should finish the reading path, provide the next action, and visually ground the design.
