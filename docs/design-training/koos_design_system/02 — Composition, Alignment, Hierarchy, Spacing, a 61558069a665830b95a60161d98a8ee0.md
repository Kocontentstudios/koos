# 02 — Composition, Alignment, Hierarchy, Spacing, and Negative Space

<aside>
📐

Composition controls where the eye begins, how it moves, what it understands, and where it finishes. Alignment and spacing should make the design feel intentional before decoration is added.

</aside>

## 1. Establish the visual skeleton

Divide the canvas into functional zones before placing detailed elements:

- Brand zone.
- Message zone.
- Hero-image zone.
- Supporting-information zone.
- Action zone.
- Footer or utility zone.

Zones may overlap, but they should not compete for the same space without a clear layering plan.

## 2. Safe margins and edge behaviour

- Keep essential text and logos away from the trim or screen edge.
- Use a consistent outer margin unless an image intentionally bleeds off-canvas.
- Decorative shapes may cross the edge; essential information should not.
- A cropped subject should exit the canvas confidently, not appear accidentally clipped.
- Do not create a narrow accidental gap between a large image and the canvas edge.

## 3. Alignment systems and image placement

### Left-aligned text

Place the headline, supporting copy, and CTA on one left axis. The hero image normally occupies the right half or lower-right.

Use when:

- The message must be read quickly.
- A person faces or gestures left.
- A product can be enlarged on the right.
- The footer contains horizontal utility information.

Keep the image from entering the text column unless the overlap is low-contrast and intentional.

### Right-aligned text

Place the image on the left and align the text to a stable right edge. Use less often because left-to-right readers scan left-aligned text more easily.

Use when:

- The subject naturally faces right.
- The brand has a strong right-aligned system.
- The composition needs variation within a campaign.

Avoid long right-aligned paragraphs.

### Centred alignment

Place logo, headline, supporting copy, and CTA on a shared centre axis. The image can sit below the text, behind it at low contrast, or symmetrically around it.

Use when:

- The message is short.
- The composition is ceremonial, celebratory, iconic, or product-led.
- The image is symmetrical or can be framed centrally.

Do not centre many unrelated details. Centring works best with a simple content stack.

### Split alignment

Assign one side to the message and the other to imagery. Use an invisible vertical divide or a visible panel.

Keep the dominant side slightly larger. A perfect 50/50 split can feel static unless symmetry is intentional.

### Grid alignment

Use repeated rows and columns for services, speakers, products, schedules, or benefits. Align card edges, image heights, labels, and internal padding.

A grid should simplify multiple items; it should not become a box around every sentence.

### Diagonal alignment

Use diagonals to imply speed and motion. Align objects along one dominant diagonal rather than several conflicting angles. Keep small text horizontal unless the brand explicitly requires rotation.

## 4. Hierarchy controls

Create hierarchy using a combination of:

- Scale.
- Weight.
- Contrast.
- Position.
- Isolation.
- Colour.
- Depth.
- Shape containment.
- Repetition.

Do not rely only on font size. A smaller isolated CTA can attract more attention than larger crowded text.

## 5. Reading-path patterns

Common paths include:

- **Z-path:** logo → headline → image → CTA/footer.
- **F-path:** headline → supporting lines → CTA, useful for text-heavy layouts.
- **Vertical stack:** logo → headline → image → action.
- **Circular path:** objects orbit a central product.
- **Diagonal path:** eye moves from an upper message toward a lower product or action.

Choose one dominant path. Decorative arrows and shapes should reinforce it, not create a second path.

## 6. Negative space

Negative space is active room that separates, groups, frames, and prioritises content.

Use it to:

- Protect the logo.
- Isolate the headline.
- Create a text zone beside an image.
- Separate the CTA from body copy.
- Prevent the footer from merging into the hero scene.
- Make a premium design feel controlled.

A space is useful when it improves grouping or attention. Empty space that traps the composition in one corner without purpose is not automatically good.

## 7. Spacing relationships

Spacing should express relationships:

- Headline lines: tight enough to read as one message.
- Headline to supporting copy: larger than internal line spacing.
- Supporting copy to CTA: enough separation to show a new function.
- Image to footer: controlled overlap or a deliberate gap, never an accidental collision.
- Contact groups: equal internal spacing and consistent separators.

Use repeatable spacing units. For example, choose a base unit and use approximately 1× for close relationships, 2× for grouped sections, and 3–4× for major section changes.

## 8. Scale and proportion

- One element should clearly dominate.
- The hero image should be large enough to communicate expression, product detail, or action.
- Logos should identify, not dominate, unless the post is specifically a brand announcement.
- Supporting copy should remain readable but subordinate.
- Footer text should be legible at actual output size.

Exaggerated scale is useful when intentional: a product near the camera can become the campaign idea. Randomly enlarging several objects creates confusion.

## 9. Overlap and layering

Overlap creates depth when the front-to-back order is believable:

1. Background atmosphere.
2. Structural shapes.
3. Distant objects.
4. Main subject or product.
5. Foreground object or accent.
6. Text and CTA where readability requires them above the scene.
7. Footer or contact layer.

Do not let hair, hands, gadgets, or decorative shapes cover important letters. Allow overlap at low-information edges rather than faces, logos, product labels, or key words.

## 10. Balance

Balance does not require symmetry. A large image on one side can be balanced by a compact but high-contrast headline on the other.

Evaluate visual weight through:

- Size.
- Darkness or brightness.
- Detail.
- Saturation.
- Human faces.
- Text density.
- Position near an edge.

If one side feels heavy, first adjust scale, spacing, or position before adding decorative objects to the opposite side.

## 11. Compact layout-selection rules

- **Long headline + one person:** left text, right image.
- **Short statement + iconic product:** centred stack.
- **Several equal services:** grid.
- **Two contrasting messages:** split layout.
- **Speed or youth concept:** diagonal structure.
- **Premium editorial message:** large negative space and restrained alignment.
- **Multiple products:** curved path, platform system, or controlled cluster.
- **Event details:** stable text column with speaker or performer on the opposite side.

## 12. Mobile-size test

At reduced size, confirm:

- The first element remains obvious.
- The headline can be read without zooming.
- The face or product remains recognisable.
- The CTA still looks actionable.
- Footer details have not collapsed.
- Small decoration has not turned into visual noise.

## Correction rules

- If crowded: remove decoration, shorten copy, increase grouping, or choose a different layout.
- If empty: enlarge the hero or headline before adding filler.
- If flat: increase scale contrast, overlap, or depth.
- If chaotic: reduce alignments and return elements to one grid.
- If unbalanced: reposition the dominant mass rather than mirroring everything.

## Core rule

> Use one alignment logic, one dominant focal point, and one readable path; variation should happen inside that structure.
